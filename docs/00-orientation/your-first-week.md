# Your first week

What happens, in order, and what you need to do. Written for someone who has never used
GitHub.

If you only read one thing, read [START-HERE.md](../../START-HERE.md). This is the
slower version, for when something is confusing.

---

## Before the first session

**1. Check your access works.** Open [Discussions](../../discussions) and post in the
welcome thread. One line is enough.

Do this **now**, not the night before the first deadline. A broken invite discovered
early is an inconvenience; discovered late it is a missed submission.

**2. Turn on notifications.** Top right of the repository → **Watch** → **All Activity**.

Without this you will miss notices. GitHub will not email you by default.

**3. Skim the [front page](../../README.md).** That is the dashboard — what is on this
week, what is due. It updates itself.

## What the pieces are

Four things, and it is worth knowing which is which:

| | What it is | You use it to |
|---|---|---|
| **Repository** | The files — sessions, briefs, reference material | Read |
| **Discussions** | Threads | Ask, answer, submit — everything you *do* |
| **Issues** | Bug reports, but for documents | Report a broken or wrong document |
| **Actions** | Automation | Nothing. It runs the bot and the dashboard |

You will spend almost all of your time in **Discussions**.

## "I can't edit anything"

Correct, and intentional. You have **read** access.

You will never push a file, make a branch, or open a pull request. Everything you
produce goes in a thread — questions, submissions, answers, reviews.

This is not a restriction that was applied to you reluctantly:

> A thread captures your approach, the wrong turn and the correction.
> A file upload captures only the final artefact.

The reasoning is the part worth reading later. Including by you, in three months, when
you have forgotten why you did it that way.

## The rhythm of a week

**Before a session** — a pre-read appears, usually 15–25 minutes. Read it. The session
is built on the assumption you have.

**The session** — live. Notes and a recording link appear afterwards.

**After** — a post-read consolidates what happened, including the parts that did not
land in the room. It is short and it is the highest-value thing to read.

**Activities** — assignments have deadlines; self-work does not. Both are listed on the
dashboard, soonest first.

**Any time** — ask. That is the whole point of the forum.

## When you get stuck

In order:

1. **Search Discussions first.** Someone has often asked already. Try
   `is:unanswered label:topic:setup` or just the error message.
2. **Check the session's post-read.** It answers the questions asked in the room.
3. **Post in Doubts — Session**, naming the session. Say what you understood, where it
   broke down, and **what you already tried** — that last one is what gets you a fast
   answer.
4. **Comment `/staff`** if the thread stalls, or it is urgent.

**Being stuck is not a mark against you.** Being silently stuck until the deadline is
the thing to avoid — by then nobody can help.

## Vocabulary

Terms that will appear before anyone explains them:

| Term | Means |
|---|---|
| **Thread** | One Discussion post and its replies |
| **Category** | The section of Discussions a thread lives in — Q&A, Assignments, Doubts |
| **Label** | A tag on a thread. `topic:retrieval`, `session:S01` |
| **Marked answer** | The reply the asker says solved their problem. Green tick |
| **Verified answer** | Staff confirmed it is *correct*. A different claim |
| **Brief** | The document describing an assignment |
| **Rubric** | How the assignment is marked. Published with the brief |
| **Pre-read / post-read** | Short documents before and after a session |
| **The bot** | Automation that acknowledges and labels. It does **not** answer questions |

## Things that surprise people

**The bot replies within a minute.** That is your confirmation the submission
registered. If it does not appear in ten minutes, something is wrong — use `/staff`.

**Your approach is asked for before your work.** Deliberate. An approach written
afterwards is a reconstruction of one.

**The rubric is published with the brief.** Not a leak. Knowing how you are assessed
makes the work better, and there is nothing gained by hiding it.

**Answering other people counts.** It is participation, not extra credit — and it is
the fastest way to find out whether you actually understood something.

**Wrong answers are not deleted.** A thread where someone was wrong and then corrected
is more useful than one that was right first time. There is a `retracted` label for
exactly this.
